│    └─ answer:
│       │ ## EU AI Act Risk Tiers
│       │ 
│       │ **Unacceptable Risk (Prohibited):** These AI systems are banned outright due to fundamental threats to rights and safety. Examples include social scoring by governments, real-time remote biometric surveillance in public spaces (with narrow exceptions), AI that exploits vulnerabilities of specific groups, and subliminal manipulation techniques that cause harm.
│       │ 
│       │ **High Risk:** Systems subject to strict obligations before market placement, including conformity assessments, transparency requirements, and human oversight. This category covers AI used in critical infrastructure, education, employment (e.g., CV-sorting tools), essential services (credit scoring, insurance), law enforcement, migration/border control, and administration of justice.
│       │ 
│       │ **Limited Risk:** Systems with specific transparency obligations but fewer restrictions — users must be informed they are interacting with AI. Chatbots, deepfake-generating systems, and emotion recognition tools fall here, where the main requirement is disclosure so users can make informed decisions.
│       │ 
│       │ **Minimal Risk:** The vast majority of AI applications (e.g., spam filters, AI in video games, recommendation systems) face no mandatory obligations under the Act, though voluntary codes of conduct are encouraged.
│    Leaf 1.2 (wall 7.82s)
│    ├─ sub-question: What are the specific compliance obligations and conformity assessment requirements for high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act?
│    └─ answer:
│       │ Under the EU AI Act, high-risk AI systems (listed in Annexes II and III) must undergo a conformity assessment before market placement, which depending on the system type is either a self-assessment by the provider or a third-party assessment by a notified body — the latter being mandatory for biometric identification systems and AI used in critical infrastructure. Providers must implement a quality management system, conduct a fundamental rights impact assessment, register the system in the EU database, and ensure technical documentation, logs, transparency measures, human oversight mechanisms, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity standards are all met before affixing CE marking. Ongoing obligations include post-market monitoring, incident reporting to national authorities, and cooperation with market surveillance authorities, while importers and distributors carry secondary compliance duties to verify that providers have fulfilled these requirements.

│  decomposed into 2 leaf sub-questions:
│    Leaf 3.1 (wall 6.81s)
│    ├─ sub-question: What is the UK government's overall AI regulatory approach and policy framework as outlined in its AI regulation white paper or strategy documents?
│    └─ answer:
│       │ The UK government's AI regulatory approach, as outlined in its 2023 AI Regulation White Paper ("A pro-innovation approach to AI regulation"), adopts a principles-based, sector-led framework rather than creating a single overarching AI regulator or binding AI-specific legislation. Instead, it assigns responsibility to existing regulators (such as the FCA, ICO, and CMA) to apply five cross-cutting principles — safety, security and robustness; transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; and contestability and redress — within their respective domains. This "light-touch" approach is explicitly designed to avoid stifling innovation while remaining adaptable, with the government reserving the option to introduce legislation if voluntary measures prove insufficient.
│    Leaf 3.2 (wall 8.99s)
│    ├─ sub-question: What are the specific roles and responsibilities of UK sector-specific regulators (such as the FCA, ICO, CMA, and Ofcom) in overseeing AI within their respective domains?
│    └─ answer:
│       │ **UK Sector-Specific Regulators and Their AI Oversight Roles**
│       │ 
│       │ The **Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)** oversees AI in financial services by ensuring firms using AI in trading, credit decisions, and customer services comply with existing principles around fairness, transparency, and consumer protection, and has established an AI Lab to test AI-related regulatory questions through its sandbox framework. The **Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)** regulates AI through the lens of data protection law (UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018), requiring organisations to conduct Data Protection Impact Assessments for high-risk AI processing, ensure lawful bases for data use, and uphold rights around automated decision-making under Article 22. The **Competition and Markets Authority (CMA)** scrutinises AI's impact on market competition and consumer protection—publishing its AI Foundation Models review (2024) to assess whether AI could entrench market power—while **Ofcom** regulates AI-driven algorithmic systems used by online platforms and broadcasters, including under the Online Safety Act 2023, which requires platforms to conduct risk assessments on algorithmic recommendation systems that may amplify harmful content.
