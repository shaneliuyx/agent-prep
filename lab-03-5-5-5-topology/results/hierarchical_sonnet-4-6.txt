│       │ The EU AI Act defines four risk tiers:
│       │ 
│       │ 1. **Unacceptable Risk (Prohibited):** AI systems banned outright, including social scoring by governments, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces (with narrow exceptions), subliminal manipulation, and exploitation of vulnerable groups.
│       │ 
│       │ 2. **High Risk:** Systems requiring strict conformity assessments before deployment, covering areas like biometric identification, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, and administration of justice.
│       │ 
│       │ 3. **Limited Risk:** Systems with transparency obligations only — users must be informed they are interacting with AI (e.g., chatbots, deepfakes).
│       │ 
│       │ 4. **Minimal Risk:** The vast majority of AI applications (e.g., spam filters, AI in video games) face no specific obligations, though voluntary codes of conduct are encouraged.
│    Leaf 1.2 (wall 8.28s)
│    ├─ sub-question: What are the specific compliance obligations and conformity assessment requirements for high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act?
│    └─ answer:
│       │ Under the EU AI Act, high-risk AI systems (listed in Annexes II and III) must undergo a conformity assessment before market placement, which for most categories can be conducted as an internal self-assessment by the provider, but for certain high-risk applications (such as biometric identification systems and AI used in critical infrastructure) requires third-party assessment by a notified body. Providers must also implement a quality management system, maintain comprehensive technical documentation, ensure human oversight measures, achieve appropriate levels of accuracy/robustness/cybersecurity, register the system in the EU database, and affix CE marking upon successful conformity assessment. Post-market, providers must conduct ongoing monitoring, report serious incidents or malfunctions to national authorities within defined timeframes (typically 15 days for serious incidents), and keep logs for at least six months (or longer for some use cases).

│       │ ## Key US Federal Executive Orders on AI Regulation and Governance
│       │ 
│       │ **Executive Order 13859 (February 2019)** — "Maintaining American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," signed by President Trump, initiated the American AI Initiative, directing federal agencies to prioritize AI R&D investment, improve access to federal data for AI training, and develop technical standards guidance through NIST.
│       │ 
│       │ **Executive Order 14110 (October 2023)** — "Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence," signed by President Biden, was the most comprehensive federal AI governance order to date, requiring developers of powerful AI systems to share safety test results with the government, directing agencies to develop AI safety standards, and establishing guidelines around privacy, civil rights, and national security implications of AI.
│       │ 
│       │ **Executive Order 14179 (January 2025)** — "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," signed by President Trump upon returning to office, revoked EO 14110, directing agencies to develop a new AI Action Plan focused on removing regulatory barriers to AI development rather than the safety-focused framework of the Biden administration.
│    Leaf 2.2 (wall 10.42s)
│    ├─ sub-question: Which US federal agencies have issued AI-specific guidelines, frameworks, or policies, and what do they require?
│    └─ answer:
│       │ ## US Federal Agencies with AI-Specific Guidelines
│       │ 
│       │ Several US federal agencies have issued AI-specific guidelines. **NIST** published the AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) in January 2023, providing voluntary guidance for organizations to manage AI risks across four core functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. **The White House/OMB** issued Memorandum M-24-10 (March 2024), requiring federal agencies to designate Chief AI Officers, conduct AI use-case inventories, and implement minimum risk management practices for rights- and safety-impacting AI. **The Department of Defense** released its AI Ethics Principles (2020) and a subsequent implementation framework requiring AI systems to be responsible, equitable, traceable, reliable, and governable.
│       │ 
│       │ Additional agencies include **the FDA**, which has issued guidance on AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), requiring predetermined change control plans and transparency; **the FTC**, which has issued guidance warning that AI-based deception or unfair practices violate Section 5 of the FTC Act; and **EEOC and DOJ**, which have issued joint guidance clarifying that AI tools used in hiring must comply with existing anti-discrimination laws (ADA, Title VII). These frameworks range from voluntary (NIST) to binding requirements (OMB M-24-10 for federal agencies).
